Food Can Help—or Harm

Common household foods are not automatically safe for pets. Learn the basics before sharing from your plate.

Everyday pet care

“Just a little” can still be dangerous.

Good intentions need good information.

This highlight represents responsible feeding: checking whether a food is appropriate before giving it to an animal.

Dogs, cats, and birds have different nutritional needs. A food that seems harmless to a person can be dangerous for a pet, so ordinary human food should not replace a species-appropriate diet.

The lists below are based on the research prepared for this project and are meant as an awareness guide, not a complete diet plan. Individual animals can have allergies, medical conditions, or special nutritional requirements. When in doubt, a veterinarian is the safest source of advice.

🐶 Dogs

  • Can eat: cooked meats such as chicken, turkey, and beef; selected vegetables such as carrots, green beans, cucumber, pumpkin, and sweet potato; selected fruits such as apple without seeds, blueberries, and bananas; plain cooked rice; and small amounts of suitable cheese.
  • Avoid: chocolate, coffee and caffeine; grapes, raisins and currants; onions, garlic, chives and leeks; xylitol; macadamia nuts; and cooked bones.

🐱 Cats

  • Can eat: appropriate cooked animal protein such as chicken, turkey and beef; small amounts of cooked fish or egg; plain pumpkin or cooked carrots; and complete formulated cat food containing essential nutrients such as taurine.
  • Avoid: onions, garlic and chives; chocolate, coffee and tea; grapes and raisins; excessive dairy; and raw yeast dough. A home-prepared diet should not replace a balanced feline diet without veterinary guidance.

🐦 Birds

  • Can eat: species-appropriate formulated pellets as a staple for many companion birds, plus suitable fresh vegetables, selected fruits with seeds/pits removed, and unsalted cooked grains or legumes as appropriate to the species.
  • Avoid: avocado, chocolate and caffeine, fruit seeds and pits, onions and garlic, uncooked/dried beans, and high-salt human snacks.
Emergency note: If a pet may have eaten a toxic substance, do not wait for symptoms and do not attempt a home remedy. Contact a veterinarian or an appropriate animal poison/emergency service as soon as possible.

Important distinction

“Can eat” does not mean “should eat freely.” Portion, preparation, species, age, health condition, and the animal's complete diet all matter.
